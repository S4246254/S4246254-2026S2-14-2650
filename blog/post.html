Edge highlighting without the chalky finish

Most edge highlights read as chalk because the last pass is too opaque and too wide. Here is the ratio I use and where to stop.

Renn Vasco Published 2 August 2026 Updated 9 August 2026 About 6 minutes to read

  • Painting
  • Beginner

An edge highlight is not a line drawn on an edge. It is a claim about where the light is coming from, and it only works if you make the same claim on every model in the unit.

Thin further than you think

The single most common problem is paint straight from the pot. At full opacity the highlight sits on top of the surface as a stripe. Thin it until it looks alarmingly transparent — roughly two parts water to one part paint — and build the brightness over two or three passes instead.

Each pass should be barely visible on its own. If the first pass is clearly there, it is too thick, and no amount of tidying afterwards will fix the chalky look.

Load the brush properly

Bring the paint to a point on the palette, then wipe most of it off. You want the paint sitting in the belly of the brush and only a trace at the tip. A size 1 with a good point will do finer work than a size 0 that has splayed.

If the brush is leaving a wet line rather than a dry-looking one, there is too much paint on it. Wipe and go again.

Only highlight what the light would hit

Pick a direction — top-left is conventional and easy to keep consistent across a unit — and highlight only the edges facing it. Highlighting every edge is the second most common mistake, and it flattens the model rather than defining it.

  • Upward-facing edges get the full highlight.
  • Side-facing edges get one thinned pass at most.
  • Downward-facing edges get nothing.

Know when to stop

Put the model at arm's length under normal room light. If the highlight is the first thing you see, it is too bright. It should read as an edge catching the light, not as a white line. Step back after every pass, because at fifteen centimetres everything looks subtle.

What I used

A size 1 sable, one pot of Bone Ivory thinned as above, and a wet palette so the mix stays consistent across a session. The Sable Brush Set covers this and the base coating in the same three brushes.
